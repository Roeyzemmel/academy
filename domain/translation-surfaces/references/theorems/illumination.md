## 1. Illumination & blocking

### 1.0 Basic definitions (as fixed by Lelièvre–Monteil–Weiss)

Use these; the literature is not fully uniform, and several results are sensitive to which
version is meant.

- **Finitely blocked pair.** "A pair of points $(x,y) \in M \times M$ is *finitely blocked* if
  there exists a finite set $B \subset M$ which does not contain $x$ or $y$ and intersects
  every straight-line trajectory connecting $x$ and $y$. A set $B$ with this property is
  called a *blocking set* for $(x,y)$, and the minimal cardinality of a blocking set is called
  the *blocking cardinality* of $(x,y)$ and is denoted by $\mathrm{bc}(x,y)$."
- **Do not illuminate each other.** $x$ and $y$ do not illuminate each other iff they are
  "finitely blocked with blocking cardinality zero, that is, if there is no straight-line path
  on $M$ from $x$ to $y$." So **illumination is the $\mathrm{bc}=0$ case of finite blocking**,
  i.e. the "empty blocking set" case. (Apisa–Wright phrase it as: illumination "is the special
  case of the finite blocking problem when the blocking set is required to be empty.")
- **Blocking property.** "A translation surface $M$ has the *blocking property* if any pair
  $(x,y) \in M \times M$ is finitely blocked, and the *bounded blocking property* if there is a
  number $n$ such that any pair $(x,y) \in M \times M$ is finitely blocked with blocking
  cardinality at most $n$."
- **Torus cover (LMW sense).** "A translation surface $M$ is a *torus cover* if there is a
  surjective translation map from $M$ to a torus (the singularities of $M$ may project to one
  or several points on the torus). Equivalently…, the subgroup of $\mathbb{R}^2$ generated by
  holonomies of absolute periods on $M$ is discrete."

**Source.** Lelièvre–Monteil–Weiss, *Everything is illuminated*, arXiv:1407.2975,
https://arxiv.org/html/1407.2975 ; published Geom. Topol. **20** (2016), no. 3, 1737–1762,
DOI 10.2140/gt.2016.20.1737 (https://projecteuclid.org/journals/geometry-and-topology/volume-20/issue-3/Everything-is-illuminated/10.2140/gt.2016.20.1737.full).
Apisa–Wright phrasing: arXiv:1708.03411, https://arxiv.org/html/1708.03411v3.

> ⚠️ **Critical notational trap.** LMW's "torus cover" allows branching over *several* points
> and is therefore **strictly weaker than "square-tiled"/"arithmetic."** By Gutkin–Judge,
> $\mathrm{SL}(X,\omega)$ is arithmetic iff $(X,\omega)$ is tiled by parallelograms (see §3.4).
> A torus cover branched over two points in generic relative position has discrete *absolute*
> periods but irrational *relative* periods; it is a torus cover in the LMW sense and need not
> be square-tiled, and need not be a lattice surface at all. LMW's Theorem 1 applies to all of
> these. Do not silently swap "torus cover" for "square-tiled" or "arithmetic."

### 1.1 Blocking dichotomy (Lelièvre–Monteil–Weiss, 2016)

**Statement.** *Theorem 1.* For a translation surface $M$, the following are equivalent:
1. $M$ is a torus cover;
2. $M$ has the blocking property;
3. There is an open set $U \subset M \times M$ such that any pair of points in $U$ is finitely blocked;
4. $M$ has the bounded blocking property.

(Verbatim: "For a translation surface $M$, the following are equivalent: (1) $M$ is a torus
cover. (2) $M$ has the blocking property. (3) There is an open set $U \subset M \times M$ such
that any pair of points in $U$ is finitely blocked. (4) $M$ has the bounded blocking property.")

**Notation used.** "Torus cover" in the LMW sense above (branching over one *or several*
points; equivalently, discrete absolute-period group).

**Common misuse.** (a) Reading "torus cover" as "square-tiled"/"arithmetic" — see the trap box
above. (b) Note the implication (3) ⇒ (1): finite blocking on a *mere open set* of pairs already
forces a torus cover; this is much stronger than requiring it for all pairs, and is the form
usually needed in applications.

**Source.** arXiv:1407.2975, Theorem 1; Geom. Topol. 20 (2016) 1737–1762.

### 1.2 Everything is illuminated (Lelièvre–Monteil–Weiss, 2016)

**Statement.** *Theorem 2.* "For any translation surface $M$, and any point $x \in M$, the set
of points $y$ which are not illuminated by $x$ is finite. Moreover, the set
$\{(x,y): x \text{ and } y \text{ do not illuminate each other}\}$ is the union of a finite set,
and of finitely many translation surfaces $M'$ embedded in $M \times M$, such that the
projections $p_i|_{M'} : M' \to M$ are both finite-degree covers."

**Notation used.** $p_1, p_2 : M \times M \to M$ the two coordinate projections.

**Common misuse — this is the big one.** The finiteness in the first sentence is **for each
fixed $x$**. The *set of unilluminated pairs* in $M \times M$ can be **infinite**: LMW
themselves write, "Hubert, Schmoll and Troubetzkoy [HST08] have constructed an example of a
translation surface $M$ which is not a torus cover, and in which there are infinitely many
pairs of points which do not illuminate each other." The "Moreover" clause is exactly what
permits this: the 2-dimensional pieces $M' \subset M \times M$ are infinite families of
mutually-non-illuminating pairs. Wolecki restates the same warning: "the statements in
Theorem 1.2 do not necessarily hold when substituting $Q$ with $M$." So: **finiteness of
pairs is a statement about rational polygons (Wolecki, §1.4), not about translation surfaces.**

**Source.** arXiv:1407.2975, Theorem 2; Geom. Topol. 20 (2016) 1737–1762. Cross-check on the
infinite-pairs example: Wolecki arXiv:1905.09358, https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1905.09358.

### 1.3 Illumination in rational polygons (Lelièvre–Monteil–Weiss, 2016)

**Statement.** *Corollary 3.* "Let $P$ be a rational polygon. Then for any $x \in P$ there are
at most finitely many points $y$ for which there is no geodesic trajectory between $x$ and $y$."

**Notation used.** "Rational polygon": all angles in $\pi\mathbb{Q}$. "Geodesic trajectory" =
billiard trajectory (reflecting off the sides).

**Common misuse.** Rationality is essential — the whole method goes through the unfolding
construction, which requires the angle group to be finite. Nothing here is known for irrational
polygons (see §5). Also: finiteness is again per source point $x$, not for the set of pairs.

**Source.** arXiv:1407.2975, Corollary 3.

### 1.4 Finiteness of unilluminated *pairs* in rational polygons (Wolecki, 2019)

**Statement.** *Theorem 1.2.* Let $Q$ be a rational polygon with connected interior and let $M$
be the translation surface obtained by unfolding $Q$. Then:
- (i) "Only finitely many pairs of points in $Q$ do not illuminate each other."
- (ii) "$\{(A,B) \in Q^2 : \mathrm{BC}(A,B) \le n\}$ is finite for every integer $n>0$ if and
  only if $M$ is not a torus cover."

Abstract (verbatim): "We show that for a rational polygonal billiard, the set of pairs of points
that do not illuminate each other (not connected by a billiard trajectory) is finite, and use
the same method to extend the results of Lelièvre, Monteil and Weiss, and of Apisa and Wright
about the amount of pairs of points that are finitely blocked with a certain blocking
cardinality. We rely on previous work about the blocking property in translation surfaces which
ultimately stems from results of Eskin, Mirzakhani and Mohammadi on dynamics of moduli spaces of
translation surfaces."

**Notation used.** "A polygon is called *rational* if all angles between edges are in
$\mathbb{Q}\pi$ where the angle between two edges is the angle at the intersection point of
their linear continuation." $\mathrm{BC}(A,B)$ = minimal cardinality of a blocking set;
$\mathrm{BC}(x,y)=0$ "amounts to non-illumination."

**Common misuse.** This upgrade from LMW Corollary 3 (finiteness per point) to finiteness of
*pairs* is genuinely polygon-specific and does **not** hold on general translation surfaces
(§1.2). Also note the hypothesis "connected interior."

**Source.** Amit Wolecki, *Illumination in Rational Billiards*, arXiv:1905.09358,
https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1905.09358 and https://arxiv.org/html/1905.09358v3.
**No journal publication was located** — treat as an arXiv preprint (see §6).

### 1.5 Small unilluminable polygons (Tokarsky 1995; Castro 1997; Wolecki 2019)

**Statement (Tokarsky).** There exists a 26-sided polygonal room with two interior points that
do not illuminate each other. Reference: G. Tokarsky, "Polygonal Rooms Not Illuminable from
Every Point," *American Mathematical Monthly* **102** (1995), no. 10, 867–879.

**Statement (Castro, 1997).** A 24-sided example, published as reader feedback in *Quantum*
magazine (per Wolecki's historical account and Wikipedia).

**Statement (Wolecki).** *Theorem 1.3.* "The minimal number of edges of a polygon with at least
one pair of points that do not illuminate each other is at most 22." Wolecki adds: "We were
recently informed by Thierry Monteil that he also found an example (unpublished) of a polygon
with 22 sides that has a pair of points that do not illuminate each other."

**Statement (Tokarsky's lemma, as generalized by Wolecki, Lemma 3.1).** For a triangle with
angle $\pi/n$ at vertex $A$ and angle $m\pi/n$ at vertex $B$, with $n$ even and $m < n-1$ a
positive integer, "there is no billiard trajectory in $Q$ from $A$ coming back to $A$."
*(This transcription of Lemma 3.1 is reported from a fetch of the arXiv HTML; see §6 —
re-read the source before quoting the exact hypotheses on $m,n$.)*

**Common misuse.** "22 is the minimum" is **not** proven. Theorem 1.3 gives only the upper
bound; the paper does not establish any lower bound and does not claim optimality.

**Source.** Wolecki arXiv:1905.09358 §3 and Theorem 1.3; Tokarsky, Amer. Math. Monthly 102
(1995) 867–879 (bibliographic data confirmed via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illumination_problem;
the Monthly article itself was not read).

### 1.6 Illumination on Veech / prelattice surfaces (Hubert–Schmoll–Troubetzkoy, 2008)

**Statement.** *Theorem 1.* "Let $X$ be a prelattice surface. Then for any points $p \in X$, the
set of points $q \in X$ which are not illuminable from $p$ is at most countable."

*Theorem 2.* "Let $X$ be a Veech surface. Then for any points $p \in X$, the set of points
$q \in X$ which are not illuminable from $p$ is finite."

*Theorem 5 (dichotomy for invariant subspaces).* "Assume $(X,\omega)$ is a Veech surface. Then
we have the following dichotomy: either $(X,\omega)$ is arithmetic and there are infinitely
many $\mathrm{Aff}^+(X,\omega)$ invariant subspaces of any even dimension in $X^n$, or
$(X,\omega)$ is not arithmetic and $X^n$ contains finitely many $\mathrm{Aff}^+(X,\omega)$
invariant subspaces."

**Notation used.**
- "A translation surface $(X,\omega)$ is *Veech* or *lattice surface*, if $\mathrm{SL}(X,\omega)$
  is a lattice in $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$."
- "A discrete group $\Gamma \subset \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ [is] called *prelattice*, if it
  contains noncommuting parabolic elements. A translation surface $(X,\omega)$ is a *prelattice
  surface* if $\mathrm{SL}(X,\omega)$ is a prelattice group."
- "A *primitive* Abelian differential $(X,\omega)$ is a translation surface which does not admit
  a translation map to a surface of lower genus."
- "For surfaces we say that $p \in X$ *illumines* $q \in X$ if there is a geodesic connecting
  $p$ to $q$."

**Common misuse.** HST Theorem 2 requires the *lattice* hypothesis. LMW's Theorem 2 is exactly
the removal of that hypothesis: LMW "extend results of Hubert–Schmoll–Troubetzkoy, removing the
hypothesis that the surface in question is a lattice surface, thus settling a conjecture" — LMW
state "The first assertion of Theorem 2 settles [HST08, Conjecture 1]." So cite HST for the
Veech case with the finer structural information (Theorems 5, 7, 8), and LMW for the general case.
Also: HST's Theorem 5 is a dichotomy about *invariant subspaces of $X^n$*, **not** the classical
Veech dichotomy about directional flows (§3.1). Do not conflate the two.

**Source.** P. Hubert, M. Schmoll, S. Troubetzkoy, *Modular Fibers and Illumination Problems*,
IMRN 2008, DOI 10.1093/imrn/rnn011; arXiv:math/0602394,
https://arxiv.org/html/math/0602394v1 and .../v2.

### 1.7 Finite blocking and pure periodicity (Monteil)

**Statement (Monteil, Ann. Inst. Fourier 2005).** Abstract, verbatim: "A planar polygonal
billiard $P$ is said to have the finite blocking property if for every pair $(O,A)$ of points in
$P$ there exists a finite number of 'blocking' points $B_1,\dots,B_n$ such that every billiard
trajectory from $O$ to $A$ meets one of the $B_i$'s. Generalizing our construction of a
counter-example to a theorem of Hiemer and Snurnikov, we show that the only regular polygons that
have the finite blocking property are the square, the equilateral triangle and the hexagon. Then
we extend this result to translation surfaces. We prove that **the only Veech surfaces with the
finite blocking property are the torus branched coverings.** We also provide a local sufficient
condition for a translation surface to fail the finite blocking property. This enables us to give
a complete classification for the L-shaped surfaces as well as to obtain a density result in the
space of translation surfaces in every genus $g \ge 2$."

**Statement (Monteil, ETDS 2009).** Abstract, verbatim: "A translation surface $\mathcal{S}$ is
said to have the finite blocking property if for every pair $(O,A)$ of points in $\mathcal{S}$
there exists a finite number of 'blocking' points $B_1,\dots,B_n$ such that every geodesic from
$O$ to $A$ meets one of the $B_i$s. $\mathcal{S}$ is said to be *purely periodic* if the
directional flow is periodic in each direction whose directional flow contains a periodic
trajectory (this implies that $\mathcal{S}$ admits a cylinder decomposition in such directions).
We will prove that the finite blocking property implies pure periodicity. We will also classify
the surfaces that have the finite blocking property in genus two: such surfaces are exactly the
torus branched coverings. Moreover, we prove that in every stratum such surfaces form a set of
null measure. In Appendix A, we prove that completely periodic translation surfaces form a set of
null measure in every stratum."

**Notation used.** "Purely periodic" as defined verbatim above — note this is *weaker* than
"completely periodic" (which requires every direction with a saddle connection to be periodic).

**Common misuse.** The 2005 result classifying finite-blocking surfaces is stated **for Veech
surfaces only**; the 2009 result classifying them is stated **in genus two only**. The
unconditional classification (all translation surfaces, all genera) is LMW Theorem 1 (§1.1).

**Source.** T. Monteil, *On the finite blocking property*, Ann. Inst. Fourier **55** (2005), no. 4,
1195–1217, arXiv:math/0406510, https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0406510 ; T. Monteil, *Finite blocking
property versus pure periodicity*, Ergodic Theory Dynam. Systems **29** (2009), no. 3, 983–996,
DOI 10.1017/S014338570800031X, arXiv:math/0406506. Also T. Monteil, *A counter-example to the
theorem of Hiemer and Snurnikov*, J. Statist. Phys. **114** (2004), no. 5–6, 1619–1623,
arXiv:math/0408431. Publication list: https://lipn.univ-paris13.fr/~monteil/papiers/index.en.html

### 1.8 Finite blocking in rational polygons — the trichotomy (Apisa–Wright, 2021)

**Statement.** *Theorem 1.1.* Let $P$ be a rational polygon.
1. "If $P$ is Gaussian or Eisenstein, any two points are finitely blocked."
2. "If $P$ is not Gaussian or Eisenstein and all its angles are multiples of $\pi/2$, then
   possibly infinitely many pairs of points are finitely blocked, but each point is finitely
   blocked from only finitely many other points."
3. "Otherwise, only finitely many pairs of points are finitely blocked in $P$."

Authors' own framing (verbatim, immediately after): "The main content is the third statement; the
first two are included for completeness and are closely related to previous results (see for
example Theorems 1 and 2 of Lelièvre, Monteil, Weiss [LMW16])."

**Notation used (verbatim definitions).**
- "We say that two, not necessarily distinct, points $x_1, x_2$ on a rational polygon are
  *finitely blocked* if there is a finite set $B$ of points such that every billiard trajectory
  from $x_1$ to $x_2$ passes through a point of $B$." (Note: **"not necessarily distinct"** —
  a point may be finitely blocked from itself.)
- "We call a polygon *Gaussian* if it can be tiled by $(\tfrac{\pi}{4},\tfrac{\pi}{4},\tfrac{\pi}{2})$
  triangles in such a way that triangles that share (part of) an edge are related via reflection.
  Similarly we call it *Eisenstein* if it can be tiled by
  $(\tfrac{\pi}{6},\tfrac{\pi}{3},\tfrac{\pi}{2})$ triangles."

**Common misuse.** Case (2) is the "all angles multiples of $\pi/2$" case: the number of finitely
blocked *pairs* may be infinite while each individual point is blocked from only finitely many
others. Quoting the theorem as a blanket "finitely many pairs" statement is wrong for cases (1)
and (2). Also, the trichotomy is stated for polygons, not translation surfaces.

**Source.** P. Apisa and A. Wright, *Marked points on translation surfaces*, arXiv:1708.03411,
https://arxiv.org/html/1708.03411v3 ; Geom. Topol. (2021)
(https://websites.umich.edu/~alexmw/papers.html).

### 1.9 Periodic points and torus covers (attributed to Eskin–Filip–Wright)

**Statement.** *Apisa–Wright Theorem 1.2.* "An affine invariant submanifold has infinitely many
periodic points if and only if it consists entirely of branched covers of tori."

**Notation used.** "Define an $\mathcal{M}$-*periodic point* to be a 1-point marking over
$\mathcal{M}$ of the same dimension as $\mathcal{M}$." Equivalently (Apisa's phrasing elsewhere):
a point that is not a zero and whose marking does not increase the dimension of the orbit closure.

**Attribution caveat (important).** Apisa–Wright attribute this to Eskin–Filip–Wright but note
"Section 4.2 explains why Theorem 1.2 is a special case of results in [EFW]." A direct search of
Eskin–Filip–Wright, *The algebraic hull of the Kontsevich–Zorich cocycle* (Ann. of Math. **188**
(2018), 281–313, arXiv:1702.02074) did **not** surface a theorem stated in terms of periodic
points. **Cite it as: "Eskin–Filip–Wright, as deduced in [Apisa–Wright, Thm 1.2 and §4.2]."**

**Source.** arXiv:1708.03411, Theorem 1.2 and §4.2. EFW: https://annals.math.princeton.edu/wp-content/uploads/annals-v188-n1-p05-p.pdf

### 1.10 Classification of markings; blocking on non-torus-cover orbit closures (Apisa–Wright)

**Statement.** *Theorem 1.3.* "Any irreducible $n$-point marking $\mathcal{N}$ over $\mathcal{M}$
with $n>1$ arises from a half-translation surface covering construction." (Stated for
$\mathcal{M}$ not a locus of torus covers.)

*Theorem 1.4.* For higher-rank invariant subvarieties of quadratic differentials: "If $Q$ is not
hyperelliptic, the only $\tilde{Q}$-periodic points are fixed points of the involution $J$";
in the hyperelliptic case periodic points also include fixed points of the hyperelliptic involution.

*Theorem 3.5 (structural).* Finitely blocked points either map to the same point under
$\pi_{Q_{\min}}$, or are periodic points / zeros in the orbit closure.

*Abstract (verbatim).* "We show that all $\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ equivariant point markings
over orbit closures of translation surfaces arise from branched covering constructions and
periodic points, completely classify such point markings over strata of quadratic differentials,
and give applications to the finite blocking problem."

**Notation used.** "We call a point marking *irreducible* if it is not reducible"; "[a marking]
is reducible when it is a fiberwise union of other point markings." "An $n$-point marking over an
affine invariant submanifold $\mathcal{M}$ [is] an affine invariant submanifold $\mathcal{N}$ of
$\mathcal{H}^*_n$ such that $\pi(\mathcal{N})$ is equal to a dense subset of $\mathcal{M}$."

**Source.** arXiv:1708.03411; https://public.websites.umich.edu/~alexmw/ApisaWright.pdf

### 1.11 Illumination / blocking on *generic* translation surfaces (Apisa, 2020)

**Statement.** *Theorem 1.4.* "A generic translation surface contains a pair of finitely blocked
points if and only if it belongs to a hyperelliptic component, in which case the pair consists of
a point and its image under the hyperelliptic involution."

*Theorem 1.5.* "If $(X,\omega)$ is a generic translation surface of genus at least two and $P$ is
a non-generic collection of points then $(X,\omega)$ belongs to a hyperelliptic component of a
stratum of Abelian differentials and $P$ contains either a Weierstrass point or two points
exchanged by the hyperelliptic involution."

*Abstract (verbatim).* "We classify $\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ invariant point markings over
components of strata of Abelian differentials. Such point markings exist only when the component
is hyperelliptic and arise from marking Weierstrass points or two points exchanged by the
hyperelliptic involution. We show that these point markings can be used to determine the
holomorphic sections of the universal curve restricted to orbifold covers of subvarieties of the
moduli space of Riemann surfaces that contain a Teichmüller disk. The finite blocking problem is
also solved for translation surfaces with dense $\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ orbit."

**Notation used.** "Generic" = the $\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ orbit is dense in its stratum
component. A point marking is "generic" if marking increases the orbit-closure dimension by $|P|$.

**Common misuse.** "Generic" here means *dense orbit in the component of the stratum*, a
measure-one but not residual-in-every-sense condition, and specifically excludes Veech surfaces
and all proper invariant subvarieties. The theorem says nothing about a given explicit surface
unless you already know its orbit closure.

**Source.** P. Apisa, *$\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})$-invariant measures in marked strata: generic
marked points, Earle–Kra for strata, and illumination*, arXiv:1601.07894,
https://arxiv.org/html/1601.07894 ; Geom. Topol. (2020).

### 1.12 Illumination/blocking on specific Veech surfaces: regular and double $n$-gons

**Statement.** *Theorem 1.3 (Apisa–Saavedra–Zhang).* "When $n \ge 5$ and $n \ne 6$, the periodic
points of the regular $n$-gon and double $n$-gon surfaces are exactly the Weierstrass points that
are not singularities of the flat metric."

*Corollary 1.6.* "When $n \ge 5$ and $n \ne 6$, the pairs of finitely blocked points on the
regular $n$-gon and double $n$-gon surfaces consist precisely of any point that is not a
singularity and its image under the hyperelliptic involution."

*Corollary 1.7.* "When $n \ge 5$ and $n \ne 6$, the
$(\pi/2,\ \pi/n,\ (n-2)\pi/2n)$ triangle admits a pair of finitely blocked points if and only if
$n$ is even, in which case the only such pair is the vertex of angle $\pi/n$ and itself."

**Notation used.** "Using the transfer principle" (their method). Note Corollary 1.7 uses the
"not necessarily distinct" convention: the blocked pair is a vertex *with itself*.

**Common misuse.** The excluded cases $n=6$ (and $n<5$) are genuine exclusions — those surfaces
are torus covers / arithmetic and have infinitely many periodic points (cf. §1.9).

**Source.** P. Apisa, R. M. Saavedra, C. Zhang, *Periodic points on the regular and double
$n$-gon surfaces*, arXiv:2011.02668, https://arxiv.org/html/2011.02668 ; Geom. Dedicata (2022),
DOI 10.1007/s10711-022-00730-6.

### 1.13 Periodic points and blocking in genus two (Apisa)

**Statement.** *Theorem 1.* "If a primitive genus two translation surface contains a periodic
point that is not a Weierstrass point then its orbit closure is the golden eigenform locus and
the periodic point is one of the golden points."

*Theorem 4.* "A singular point on a primitive genus two translation surface is finitely blocked
from no other point and a nonsingular point is only finitely blocked from its image under the
hyperelliptic involution."

*Abstract (verbatim).* "We show that all $\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{R})$-equivariant point markings
over orbit closures of primitive genus two translation surfaces arise from marking pairs of
points exchanged by the hyperelliptic involution, Weierstrass points, or the golden points in the
golden eigenform locus."

**Notation used.** "Primitive": does not admit a translation map to a lower-genus surface (so
torus covers are excluded). $\Omega E_5$ = golden eigenform locus (discriminant $D=5$).

**Common misuse.** Theorem 4 requires **primitive**; genus-two torus covers behave completely
differently (blocking property holds for all pairs, by §1.1).

**Source.** P. Apisa, *Periodic points in genus two: holomorphic sections over Hilbert modular
varieties, Teichmüller dynamics, and billiards*, arXiv:1710.05505, https://arxiv.org/html/1710.05505 ;
IMRN (to appear as of the author's page).

### 1.14 Periodic points on Veech surfaces (Möller, 2006)

**Statement.** *Abstract (verbatim).* "Periodic points are points on Veech surfaces, whose orbit
under the group of affine diffeomorphisms is finite. We characterize those points as being
torsion points if the Veech surfaces is suitably mapped to its Jacobian or an appropriate factor
thereof. For a primitive Veech surface in genus two we show that the only periodic points are the
Weierstraß points and the singularities."

**Notation used.** Here "periodic point" = finite $\mathrm{Aff}^+(X,\omega)$-orbit. This is the
Veech-surface special case of the $\mathcal{M}$-periodic point notion in §1.9.

**Common misuse.** The clean classification (Weierstrass points + singularities) is stated for
**primitive genus two** Veech surfaces only. In higher genus, Möller gives the torsion
characterization, not an explicit list.

**Source.** M. Möller, *Periodic points on Veech surfaces and the Mordell–Weil group over a
Teichmüller curve*, Invent. Math. **165** (2006), no. 3, 633–649, DOI 10.1007/s00222-006-0510-3.
