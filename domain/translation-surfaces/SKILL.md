# Translation surfaces and illumination

This skill carries the domain's notation, its theorem statements with real
hypotheses, and the specific ways people get it wrong. The reference sheet is the
substance; this file is how to use it.

Companion skills, when the session has them: **flatsurf-computation** for actually
running experiments (sage-flatsurf / surface_dynamics), **math-proof-writing** for
proof discipline, **latex-paper-writing** for the manuscript.

## Use the reference sheet, not memory

`references/theorems.md` (~1100 lines, 33 entries with sources) holds precise
statements. **Read the relevant entry before stating or citing any theorem from
this area.** Memory reconstructs hypotheses plausibly and wrongly, and in this
field the hypotheses are the content — "torus cover", "Veech", "primitive",
"prelattice", and "square-tiled" are five different conditions that get conflated.

## The traps that cost the most

These four account for most errors in this area. They are elaborated in the
sheet, but they are worth carrying in working memory.

**"Torus cover" ≠ "square-tiled" ≠ "arithmetic".** In Lelièvre–Monteil–Weiss a
torus cover may branch over *several* points; equivalently the group generated by
*absolute* period holonomies is discrete. Relative periods can be irrational, so
such a surface need not be square-tiled and need not even be a lattice surface.
LMW's blocking dichotomy (§1.1) is about this weaker class. Swapping in
"square-tiled" inverts the content of the theorem.

**Finiteness is per source point, not for pairs.** "Everything is illuminated"
says: for each fixed $x$, the set of $y$ not illuminated by $x$ is finite. The set
of unilluminated *pairs* in $M \times M$ can be infinite — Hubert–Schmoll–Troubetzkoy
built a non-torus-cover with infinitely many such pairs, and LMW's "moreover"
clause (finitely many embedded surfaces $M' \subset M\times M$) is precisely what
allows it. Finiteness of pairs is a theorem about *rational polygons* (Wolecki),
not about translation surfaces.

**Marked points change the stratum and are never harmless.** A marked point is a
zero of order 0; adding one moves $\mathcal{H}(2)$ to $\mathcal{H}(2,0)$, changes
the dimension, and changes what "the orbit closure" means. Much of the
Apisa–Wright illumination machinery is literally a theory of marked points, so in
this area the bookkeeping is the mathematics. When unfolding a billiard, check
whether the construction produced marked points or a cover rather than the
primitive surface.

**Illumination is the $\mathrm{bc}=0$ case of finite blocking.** Two points fail
to illuminate each other exactly when there is *no* straight-line path between
them — the empty-blocking-set case. Results about finite blocking with $\mathrm{bc}
\ge 1$ do not transfer automatically, and vice versa.

## Standing habits for this area

- **Say which surface.** $(X,\omega)$, the unfolding $M$ of a polygon $P$, and a
  translation cover of $M$ are three different objects and statements routinely
  fail to transfer between them. Name the one in play in every claim.
- **Check the dimension count.** $\dim_{\mathbb{C}} \mathcal{H}(k_1,\dots,k_n) =
  2g + n - 1$ in period coordinates, with $\sum k_i = 2g-2$. A claim that
  contradicts this arithmetic is wrong, and this is the cheapest available check.
- **Test on the standard examples before believing anything.** The square torus;
  a square-tiled surface in $\mathcal{H}(2)$ (the 3-square L); the regular
  pentagon / double pentagon; the Veech surfaces from the $(n,m,k)$ triangles;
  the Eierlegende Wollmilchsau. If a proposed statement already fails on the
  torus, stop. Use **flatsurf-computation** to run these.
- **Genericity language is not decoration.** "Generic" means a specific thing
  (full measure, or dense $G_\delta$, or outside a proper affine invariant
  submanifold) and results differ on which. State which one is meant.
- **Watch what unfolding does.** Unfolding a rational polygon with angle
  denominators $N$ produces a specific surface with a specific $\mathbb{Z}/N$
  symmetry; the billiard flow corresponds to the straight-line flow, and
  trajectories through corners are undefined rather than reflected. Statements
  about the polygon and about its unfolding are related by a dictionary, not by
  identity.

## Figures

`references/tikz-figures.md` has conventions and reusable TikZ for the pictures
this subject needs: polygons with edge identifications, unfoldings, cylinder
decompositions, billiard trajectories, and blocked or unilluminated point pairs.
Consistency across a paper's figures matters more than any individual picture —
same arrow marks for identified edges, same shading for cylinders, same marks for
singularities and marked points throughout.
